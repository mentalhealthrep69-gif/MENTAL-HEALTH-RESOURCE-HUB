## 1. Warning Signs of Emotional Distress

Look out for big, lasting changes in your friend's behavior rather than trying to diagnose them.

### Steps to Notice
1. **Notice Social Withdrawal:** They suddenly stop hanging out, responding to messages, or joining activities.
2. **Watch Daily Habits:** Look for major changes in how they sleep, eat, or take care of personal hygiene.
3. **Spot Ongoing Mood Shifts:** They seem constantly sad, scared, angry, or hopeless.
4. **Listen to Hopeless Words:** They say things like *"I can't do this anymore"* or *"Nothing matters."*
5. **Watch for Harmful Coping:** They start using alcohol, drugs, or risky behaviors to deal with stress.
6. **Notice Unusual Actions:** They give away valuable possessions or say unusual goodbyes.

> **Remember:** One sign alone does not mean a crisis. Look for major changes that last over time.

## 2. Signs of Suicidal Thoughts or Behavior

Treat these warning signs with serious, direct attention.

### Steps to Notice & Act
1. **Listen for Direct Danger Statements:** They talk about wanting to die, disappear, or say people *"would be better off without me."*
2. **Watch for Dangerous Actions:** They look for ways to hurt themselves or suddenly give away prized items.
3. **Notice Sudden Mood Shifts:** They suddenly seem calm or happy after a long period of deep depression.
4. **Ask Directly:** Ask them calmly: *"Are you thinking about ending your life?"* *(Asking this does NOT put the idea into their head).*
5. **Get Immediate Help:** If they are in danger, **do not leave them alone**. Contact campus security, a counselor, or emergency services right away.

## 3. How to Help a Friend (4-Step Action Plan)

Use this simple **NOTICE → ASK → LISTEN → CONNECT** plan when you are worried about someone.

### Step 1: NOTICE
* Speak up when you see behavior changes.  
* **Say:** *"I've noticed you've been very quiet lately."*

### Step 2: ASK
* Check in with them in a private, quiet place.  
* **Say:** *"Are you okay? Do you want to talk about it?"*

### Step 3: LISTEN
* Give them time to speak. Do not rush to fix their problems or judge them.  
* **Say:** *"Thank you for telling me. That sounds really hard."*

### Step 4: CONNECT
* Encourage them to speak with a professional.  
* **Say:** *"Would you like me to help you set up a meeting with a campus counselor?"*  
* *If they are in immediate danger, stay with them and call for emergency help.*

## 4. When Professional Help Is Needed

Encourage your friend (or yourself) to see a counselor or doctor when:

### Key Triggers
1. **Difficult feelings last long:** Painful emotions last more than 2 weeks or keep getting worse.
2. **Daily life is broken:** Stress stops them from attending classes, sleeping, or eating.
3. **Harmful habits appear:** They turn to self-harm, drinking, or drugs to survive the day.
4. **Peer support isn't enough:** They continue to struggle even after friends try to help.

> **Remember:** You do not have to wait for a crisis to ask for help. Getting counseling is a sign of self-care, not weakness.

## 5. What NOT to Say (And What to Say Instead)

Well-meaning words can accidentally make someone feel worse. Use these helpful alternatives instead:

| ❌ What NOT to Say | ✅ What to Say Instead |
| :--- | :--- |
| *"Just be positive."* | *"That sounds really difficult. I am here to listen."* |
| *"Other people have bigger problems."* | *"What you are going through matters."* |
| *"You'll get over it soon."* | *"You don't have to handle this alone."* |
| *"Why are you acting like this?"* | *"I noticed you've been struggling. Want to talk?"* |
| *"I promise I won't tell anyone."* | *"I respect your privacy, but if you're in danger, I have to get help to keep you safe."* |

---

> 🌱 **Main Lesson:** You do not need to be a trained counselor. Your job as a friend is simple: **Notice**, **Listen**, **Show Care**, and **Connect** them to help.
